Service identification · SOA modeling

An observatory for a living monolith.

Meridian models Harborline, a fictional legacy terminal operating system, as an existing-application case study. It triangulates service candidates from the business domain, the assets already in place, and the outcomes the port needs—then makes ownership, contracts, runtime conversations, and migration seams inspectable.

Case-study boundary Harborline, its stack, measurements, and operating facts are illustrative assumptions—not claims about a real port or an audited production system. Replace them with discovery evidence before using this model for an implementation.

    Three meridians, one catalog.

    Domain decomposition (top-down), existing-asset analysis (bottom-up), and goal-service modeling (meet-in-the-middle) each cast a vote. A candidate needs at least two votes to be promoted; cohesion, coupling, reuse, and vote coverage provide a separate ranking score.

    Promotion rule At least 2 of 3 meridian votes Scores rank candidates; they do not override the vote threshold.

    Decompose marine, landside, yard, finance. Process tasks become service seeds. Entities that own data become entity services. We refuse god-modules.

    Votes: D domain · A assets · G goals. Score = cohesion 45% + (1−coupling) 30% + reuse 15% + vote ratio 10%.

    SOA model

    The service sky.

    Process services orchestrate. Entity services own truth. Task services do one job well. Utility services are the tide that every ship rides. Edges are runtime conversations, not database foreign keys.

    Runtime conversations

    A caller depends on a service contract or event—not another service’s tables.

      Service specification

      What a service owes.

      Each contract names the layer, the data it is allowed to own, and the operations others may call. Ownership is the whole point of identification: if two services write the same box position, you still have a monolith.
